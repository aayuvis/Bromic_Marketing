# Planning Your Outdoor Heat: Professional Edition

The best outdoor spaces hold their guests long after the sun has gone, and the warmth that keeps them there is chosen early. Bromic's electric heaters are medium wave, the band that covers the most of a space, lifts the temperature furthest and carries its heat furthest, with no intrusive glow [[VERIFY: medium wave test data for heat coverage — Product team]] [[VERIFY: medium wave test data for temperature rise — Product team]] [[VERIFY: medium wave test data for distance heat travels — Product team]] [[VERIFY: medium wave test data for no intrusive visual glow — Product team]]. Medium wave warms in about 30 seconds; long wave takes about 5 minutes and is the most sensitive to wind[^s3]. Make that choice in schematic design, together with the fuel, the mounting and the load, in the order your drawing set needs it.

Fewer than one in four homeowners prioritize heating at planning[^s1]. When heat arrives late, it tends to arrive as surface conduit, a ceiling that no longer matches and a substitution at bid. Drawn into the set from schematic design, the same heater stays exactly as you specified it, from the line item to the circuit and the recess.

## Step 1. Confirm The Space Is Outdoors

For heating design, a space counts as outdoor when at least 30% of its horizontal perimeter is permanently open. A partial overhead cover holds a gentle heat cloud that adds a convective contribution; sizing always rests on radiant coverage[^s3].

| Configuration | Qualifies | Design note |
|---|---|---|
| Walls all round, one permanent ground-level opening, no roof | Yes, if the opening is 30% or more of the perimeter | No heat cloud; size for exposure |
| Overhead cover, two open sides | Yes | Heat cloud under cover |
| Overhead cover, three walls, 30% or more open | Yes | Strong heat cloud; check clearances |
| Retractable glazing or screens that close below 30% | Check | Treat as enclosed; confirm with the AHJ and Bromic |

## Step 2. Map The Heat Before The Ceiling Fills Up

Start with a heat map. Mark where people will sit, dine, stand and pass through, then lay the heat over those zones before you choose a finish or a mount, so comfort is built into the layout from the start[^s25]. Heaters compete for the same soffit as downlights, fans, speakers and ducts, and coordinating them with your MEP and lighting consultants in design development avoids rework once the reflected ceiling plan is full[^s25].

Fewer, higher-output heaters placed where they work usually serve a space better than many small ones, and read as a calmer ceiling. Match the finish to the surrounding materials, from black and white to stainless and the Select graphite, so the heater belongs to the architecture[^s25][^s2].

## Step 3. Choose The Fuel And Wavelength

### How Heat Moves Outdoors

| Mode | How it moves heat | Outdoors |
|---|---|---|
| Convection | Warms the air, which rises and is replaced by cooler air | Rises and blows away; works only where air is contained |
| Conduction | Through direct contact, surface to surface | Warms only what a guest touches |
| Radiant (infrared) | Electromagnetic waves absorbed by people and surfaces | Warms people directly and holds up in a light breeze |

Heat transfer: Bromic guidance[^s25]. In the open air, convected heat rises and disperses, forced air cannot hold an open space at temperature, and outdoor heat cannot be thermostatically controlled, so every outdoor scheme rests on radiant warmth aimed at people[^s3].

The way a heater sends its energy matters as much as the band it emits. An indirect heater, such as a mushroom heater, radiates in every direction and loses much of its output upward; a directional heater sends most of its output toward the occupied zone, so more of the energy reaches the people sitting beneath it[^s25][^s3].

| | Heat coverage | Temperature rise | Distance heat travels | Visible glow | Warm-up |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Short wave | Narrower | Lower | Shorter | Intense glow and glare | Instant[^s3] |
| **Medium wave: Bromic Platinum, Eclipse and Tungsten Electric** | **Widest** | **Greatest** | **Furthest** | **Soft, no intrusive glow** | **About 30 seconds[^s3]** |
| Long wave | Narrower | Lower | Shortest; most affected by wind[^s3] | – | About 5 minutes[^s3] |

### Detailing The Recess

A recessed heater gives warmth that is felt without being seen, and keeps the soffit line clean[^s25]. Recessing is an electric detail: Platinum and Tungsten Electric recess with a recess kit, and the Coastal and Select collections add low-clearance recessing for shallow cavities, in retrofits as well as new build[^s2][^s25]. Built-in gas heaters mount to the surface of a wall or ceiling.

Draw each recess as its own detail, coordinated with the structure and the RCP: the kit and cut-out, the cavity depth above the finished ceiling, blocking or framing around the opening, the junction-box position and its access, and the clearance the manual sets to the structure and to combustibles. ## Step 5. Size And Place Each Zone

Heaters per zone = zone area ÷ effective coverage per heater, rounded up. The [A03 calculator](asset:A03) applies its stated adjustments to the nominal coverage above, namely open space 0.85, partially covered 1.0, covered 1.1; mild 1.0, cool 0.85, cold 0.7; mounting to 10 ft 1.0, to 12 ft 0.9, above 12 ft 0.8 (built-in heaters). These are working assumptions awaiting Bromic sign-off; Bromic has not published them as ratings. [[VERIFY: A03 enclosure, climate and height factors — Product team]] Freestanding heaters (Tungsten Freestanding Gas, Eclipse Freestanding) also serve no more than four seats each, because seated guests block their radiant heat, so count max(area ÷ effective coverage, seats ÷ 4), allowing 20 ft² a seat where the seat count is unknown.

The factors exist because the same heater covers less in colder air, and wind draws heat out of the zone. On an exposed site, consider glass screens or other windbreaks on the windward side as part of the heating design[^s25].

**Worked example.** A 16 × 24 ft covered dining terrace in a cool climate, with a 10 ft soffit: 384 ft². Tungsten Electric 6000 W: 160 × 1.1 × 0.85 × 1.0 = 150 ft² effective; 384 ÷ 150 = 2.6, so **three heaters**, each on its own Affinity Dimmer channel (a 5-channel controller), a wall switch and the app.

### Mounting Height And Placement

Radiant intensity falls with the square of the distance: double the distance from the heater and about a quarter of the heat reaches the guest[^s25]. Mount too high and much of the output is lost before it arrives; too low and a seat beneath it runs hot. A seated guest's head is about 4 ft above the floor, so set heights by how each zone is used.

| Zone use | Mounting height (Bromic guidance) | Distance to a guest's head |
|---|---|---|
| Seated at standard tables | About 8 ft | About 4 ft |
| High-tops and bar rails | About 10 ft | About 4 ft |
| Standing areas, entries and walkways | About 12 ft | About 6 ft |

Mounting heights by use: Bromic guidance[^s25], never below each model's minimum (Step 8). [[VERIFY: mounting heights by use against each model's installation manual and the A03 height factors — Product team]]

Wall-mounted heaters project their heat outward, so a table pushed against the wall directly beneath one sits in a dead zone. Set the table forward into the heat, and for wider tables place heaters on both sides so every guest is in the zone; the same applies to the distance from the wall to the back of each seat[^s25].

### Writing The Sequence Of Operation

On a project with a BMS, decide early who runs the heat. Because BMS mode disables the app[^s17], the controls narrative should state one of two paths: the app and wall switches run schedules and overrides, or the BMS drives each zone through the expansion board's Ethernet or 0–10 V interface, with the wall switch kept as the local override. Coordinate the interface with the BMS and lighting-control integrators, and show the controller, its data and power runs, and each zone's channel on the controls drawing.
