# The outdoor heating planning guide

*Operator edition, for restaurants, hotels, bars and venues. Extend the Moment.*

Treat patio heat as revenue infrastructure, not a comfort accessory. Heated seats stay sellable on cold evenings and in the shoulder months. Choose the fuel on five-year cost rather than purchase price, zone and schedule the heat so it runs only when seats are full, clear the fire code before the first heater arrives, and standardise the package so every site is built the same way.

Outdoor seating is mainstream: in a 2020 National Restaurant Association survey, 74% of full-service operators offered it, and 49% were taking steps to extend the outdoor season, including tents or patio heaters[^s1]. The question is not whether to heat, but how to make the heat pay.

## Step 1. What is a heated seat worth?

The revenue case is arithmetic on your own numbers:

**Added revenue = heated seats × turns per night × average check × extra operating nights per year × capture rate**

**Payback (months) = all-in installed cost ÷ (added contribution per year ÷ 12)**

Use contribution margin, not revenue, for payback. Count only the nights you could not sell the patio without heat: cold evenings, shoulder seasons, and late seatings after sunset.

| Input | Where to get it |
|---|---|
| Heated seats | Floor plan |
| Turns per night | POS, outdoor sections only |
| Average check | POS |
| Extra nights per year | Last year's closures or empty patio nights by weather |
| Capture rate | Share of those nights you expect to fill; be conservative |

## Step 2. Define the space

- **Outdoor means at least 30% of the perimeter permanently open**[^s3]. Enclosing a patio with walls, glazing or plastic sidewalls can change both the heating design and what the fire code permits (Step 6).
- **A cover helps.** A partial roof or awning holds a heat cloud that adds to the radiant heat[^s3].
- **Radiant is the only approach that works outdoors.** It warms guests and surfaces, not air, so it holds up in a light breeze; forced air and thermostats do not work in open space[^s3].
- **Map seats, not square feet.** Draw heat over tables and the bar rail; service aisles need less.

## Step 3. Which fuel costs least over five years?

Purchase price is the smallest number in the decision. Over five years, energy, handling and labour usually decide it.

### The five-year TCO logic

**5-year TCO = hardware + controls + installation + permits + (5 × annual energy) + (5 × annual handling) + maintenance**

- **Annual energy** = operating hours × cost per hour
- **Annual handling** (propane): cylinder deliveries or swaps, staff time, storage and its compliance
- **Normalise by heated area**, not per heater: a gas freestanding unit covers about 215 ft²; electric models 65–143 ft² each[^s2]

### Cost per hour

Formulas: electric $/h = kW × $/kWh · natural gas $/h = Btu/h ÷ 100,000 × $/therm · propane $/h = Btu/h ÷ 91,500 × $/gal.

[[VERIFY: 2026 rates — Brian Kim/Finance]] US average rates used: commercial electricity 12.75¢/kWh (2024)[^s4]; commercial natural gas $10.07/Mcf (2024)[^s5], about $0.97 per therm[^s6]; propane $2.46/gal (EIA residential winter 2025–26 forecast; commercial delivered prices differ)[^s7].

|---|---|---|---|

## Step 5. Zones, schedules and controls (required)

Controls are what make the operating cost in Step 3 real. Without them, heat runs at full output until someone remembers to switch it off.

| Need | Control |
|---|---|
| Heat one section while another is closed | Zones, one Affinity controller per zone |
| On before service, off at close | Schedules and timers in the Bromic Smart-Heat app |
| Staff override at the station | Manual wall switch |
| Lower output on mild nights | Dimmer or Eclipse controller (33/66/100%) |
| Central control across a venue | Control4, Crestron or Lutron integration |

Wi-Fi and app features require compatible Affinity Smart-Heat Controls, sold separately. Integration compatibility varies by system and may need additional hardware or configuration[^s2]. [[VERIFY: multi-site or remote management capability of the app; BMS/BACnet support — Mike Clifford]]

**Set it up like this:** one zone per section that opens and closes on its own; schedules tied to service hours; a lower step for shoulder nights; the wall switch for the closing manager.

## Step 6. Permits and fire code

Start with the fire marshal, not the supplier. Rules are adopted and amended locally.

| Topic | Primary source | Pointer |
|---|---|---|
| Portable unvented fuel-fired heaters | International Fire Code 603.4[^s10] | Prohibited in Group A (assembly, including restaurants) and several other occupancies; outdoor gas-fired heaters fall under their own subsection with conditions[^s8] |
| Outdoor portable gas heaters | IFC 603.4.2 as locally adopted | One local interpretation bars them inside tents, membrane structures and on balconies, and requires 5 ft from buildings, combustible overhangs and exits[^s9] |
| Enclosed patios | IFC 603.4 interpretation | North Carolina's fire marshal: a restaurant patio enclosed with clear plastic needs a vented fuel-fired heater or an electric heater[^s8] |
| LP-gas cylinder storage | NFPA 58[^s11] | Storage location, quantity and handling of spare cylinders |
| Electric installation | NFPA 70[^s12] | Circuits, disconnects, wiring methods |
| Gas installation | NFPA 54[^s12] | Fixed gas supply, clearances, ventilation |
| Certification | Intertek, UL or CSA mark | Required on gas and electrical appliances, including portable heaters[^s3] |
| Electrification and gas restrictions | Local ordinance | See the Electrification compliance guide ([A12](asset:A12)) |

[[VERIFY: current IFC 2024 text of 603.4 and 603.4.2 — Shaughn Ritchie]]

*This table points to primary sources; it is not legal advice. The locally adopted code and the AHJ's interpretation govern. Confirm with the local building and fire authority, and use a licensed electrician or gas fitter.*

## Step 7. Roll out across sites

1. **Standardise the package.** One heater family, one controller set-up, one mounting detail per site type (canopy, wall, freestanding).
2. **Survey each site the same way.** Seats, perimeter openness, ceiling height, supply voltage, panel capacity, gas availability, Wi-Fi.
3. **Clear permits in parallel.** Each jurisdiction separately; build the slowest into the programme.
4. **Pilot, measure, then scale.** Track covers and sales on heated nights against the ROI case before the next wave.
5. **Name an owner per site** for schedules and seasonal changeover.

**Multi-site survey checklist**
- [ ] Heated seats and zones drawn
- [ ] ≥ 30% perimeter permanently open; enclosure plans noted
- [ ] Fuel available: supply voltage (208/240/277 V), panel capacity, natural gas
- [ ] Heater model, count and mounting per zone
- [ ] Affinity controller per zone; wall switch; app and Wi-Fi checked
- [ ] Fire-marshal and building-permit path confirmed
- [ ] Five-year TCO run ([A20](asset:A20)); ROI case run ([A21](asset:A21))
- [ ] Bromic design service layout requested (photos, plans or drawings)
