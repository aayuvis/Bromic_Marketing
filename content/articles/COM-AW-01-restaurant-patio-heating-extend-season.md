Restaurants keep outdoor dining open in winter by heating the guest, not the air. Radiant (infrared) heaters warm people and tables directly, so they keep working in a light breeze; add a partial cover and some wind screening, and a patio that closes in October can sell seats on most cool evenings.[^s3] The decision is a revenue one: every heated seat is a seat you can sell on a night it would otherwise sit empty.

## Why do patio seats sit empty for half the year?

Because guests get cold before the kitchen closes. A patio is often a large share of capacity and of income. In a 2021 National Restaurant Association survey, 65% of operators offered outdoor dining, and 35% of those said it made up more than 40% of average daily sales — 41% among full-service restaurants.[^s1] Demand has held: the Association's 2023 industry report found 69% of adults like the option to eat outside.[^s2] [[VERIFY: confirm the 69% figure in the NRA 2023 State of the Restaurant Industry report itself, or cut it — Megan Spies]]

When the evening turns, those seats stop earning. The loss is quiet: no complaint, just a host steering guests indoors or a patio closed for the season.

## What actually keeps a guest warm outside?

Outdoors, warm air rises and blows away. Forced-air heating cannot hold a temperature in an open space, and a thermostat has nothing to read. Radiant heat behaves like sunshine: it lands on people and surfaces.[^s3]

Not all radiant heat is equal. Bromic's planning guidance groups it by wavelength:[^s3]

| Heat source | Share radiant | Warm-up | Wind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short wave | about 90% | instant | warms people directly |
| Medium wave | about 60% | about 30 seconds | limited sensitivity |
| Long wave | about 40% | about 5 minutes | most easily dispersed |

For service, warm-up time matters. A heater that is warm the moment a table is seated can be switched by zone, not left running all night.

## Which patios can be heated well?

Most of them, if the design is right. Bromic treats a space as outdoor when at least 30% of its perimeter is permanently open. A partial cover — an awning, pergola or soffit — traps a "heat cloud" that adds a convective boost on top of the radiant heat.[^s3]

- **Covered terraces and awnings:** mount electric heaters on the wall or soffit, out of the way of service.
- **Open courtyards and sidewalks:** freestanding heaters move with the layout; allow one for every four seats.
- **Rooftops:** wind is the enemy; screen the windward side and favour short-wave heat.

Coverage varies with model, size and mounting height; cold, exposed sites need more units for the same area. A freestanding heater serves no more than four seats, whatever its coverage, because seated guests block its radiant heat: 40 seats need at least ten. Wi-Fi and app features — zones, schedules and timers in the Bromic Smart-Heat app — require compatible Affinity Smart-Heat Controls, sold separately.[^s4]

## How do operators keep the running cost in check?

By heating only the seats in use. Outdoor heat cannot be thermostatically controlled, so control is the cost lever:[^s3]

1. Split the patio into zones that match sections and servers.
2. Schedule zones to the reservation book, not the clock.
3. Use output steps — Eclipse runs at 33, 66 or 100% — to match the night.[^s4]
4. Switch off a zone when the last table leaves.

Fuel choice matters too. Propane is cheap to buy into and costly to run and handle; natural gas and electric trade differently. See [the five-year cost comparison](item:COM-CO-01).

## How do you know it will pay?

Put a number on the empty nights. The method is simple: heated seats × turns × average check × extra nights × the share of those seats you actually fill. Set that against the installed cost and running cost, and you have a payback in months. [The ROI article](item:COM-IN-01) walks through it with every input labelled.

Before you buy, check the rules. Gas heaters near buildings, exits and awnings, propane storage and sidewalk permits are all regulated locally — see [the permit and fire-code checklist](item:COM-PP-01).

## Key takeaways

- Radiant heat warms guests, not air, so it works where forced air cannot.
- A partial cover and wind screening multiply what each heater delivers.
- Patios carry real revenue: for a third of operators with outdoor seating it is more than 40% of daily sales.[^s1]
- Zones and schedules, through compatible Affinity controls, keep running cost tied to covers.
- Make the case with numbers before the season, not after.

## FAQ

### Can a restaurant patio stay open all winter?

On many nights, yes, with radiant heat, a partial cover and wind screening. In very cold or windy climates expect a shorter extension, and plan more heaters per square foot. Bromic's design service can model your site.

### Do patio heaters work in wind?

Radiant heaters do, within limits, because they warm people rather than air. Short-wave heat is the least affected; long-wave heat is the most.[^s3] Screening the windward side helps any heater.

### Electric or gas for a restaurant patio?

Electric suits covered, fixed installs and avoids fuel handling; gas suits large open areas with a gas supply. Propane brings tank storage and exchange labour. Compare five-year cost, not purchase price.

### How many heaters does a patio need?

For overhead heaters, divide the seating area (about 20 ft² a seat) by each heater's coverage adjusted for cover and climate, and round up: 40 seats, partly covered on cool evenings, is 800 ÷ (143 × 0.85) = 6.6, so seven Platinum 4500W heaters with Affinity Smart-Heat On/Off or Dimmer controls and a wall switch. Freestanding heaters serve no more than four seats each, so the same 40 seats need ten. See [how many heaters for a 40-seat patio](item:COM-IN-KB1).

### Do I need a permit for patio heaters?

Often. Hard-wired electric heaters usually need an electrical permit, gas lines a plumbing or fuel-gas permit, and portable gas heaters must meet the fire code. Confirm with your local building and fire authority.
