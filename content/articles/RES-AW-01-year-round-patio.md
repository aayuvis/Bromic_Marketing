To use a patio when it's cold, do three things: cut the wind, put something overhead, and warm the people rather than the air. Radiant (infrared) heaters do the last part — they warm skin and furniture the way the sun does, so they keep working in an open space where warm air would simply blow away.[^s4] Plan all three together and an evening outside lasts hours longer, from autumn into spring.

## Why do so many patios sit empty after dark?

Because the patio was built and the heat was not. Most new homes now come with one: 60.5% of single-family homes started in 2025 had a patio, and the average new-home patio is about 320 ft².[^s1][^s2] Yet in Bromic's own homeowner survey, 94% said their outdoor space could be used more, and fewer than one in four (23%) put heating into the plan at the start.[^s3] [[VERIFY: survey base, fieldwork dates and question wording for the 94% and 23% figures — Megan Spies]]

The result is familiar. Dinner moves inside at sunset. The lounge set is used in July and covered in October. The fix is not more furniture; it is comfort that holds when the temperature drops.

## What actually works to keep a patio warm?

Each layer does a different job. The table ranks them by what they change.

| Layer | What it does | Works in wind? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind break (screen, wall, planting) | Stops moving air stripping heat off skin | Yes — that is its job | Keep the space outdoor: at least 30% of the perimeter permanently open[^s4] |
| Overhead cover (pergola, roof, louvres) | Holds a "heat cloud" over the seating and adds a convective boost[^s4] | Partly | Also gives a mounting surface for built-in heaters |
| Radiant heater, short or medium wave | Warms people and objects directly | Yes, in light to mild wind[^s4] | Short wave warms almost instantly; medium wave in about 30 seconds[^s4] |
| Fire pit or fireplace | Atmosphere and a local hot spot | Poorly | Warms whoever faces it; smoke follows the breeze |
| Forced-air or convection heater | Heats air | No | Bromic's guidance: forced air does not work outdoors[^s4] |
| Blankets, cushions, warm lighting | Comfort at the edges | n/a | Best alongside heat, not instead of it |

## Why is radiant heat the right kind for outdoors?

Outdoors, there is no box of air to warm. Anything that heats air loses it to the first breeze. Infrared heat travels as radiation and is absorbed by what it reaches — your shoulders, the table, the stone floor — which then stays warm.[^s4]

Not all infrared is equal. Bromic's planning guide splits it by wavelength: short wave is about 90% radiant and warms instantly; medium wave about 60% radiant with a roughly 30-second warm-up; long wave only about 40% radiant, around five minutes to warm up, and the most affected by wind.[^s4] For a patio people use on a whim, warm-up time matters.

## When should heat go into the plan?

Before anything is built. Heat is cheapest and best-looking when it is designed from the start, not added later:

- **Wiring and gas** are simplest to run while walls and ceilings are open. Adding them later can mean channelling stone or patching a finished soffit.
- **Recessed heaters** need a cavity planned into the ceiling; recess kits flush-mount electric units into it.[^s4]
- **Mounting heights** set where heaters can go. Bromic guidance: wall mounts at least 72" above the floor, ceiling mounts at least 96", varying with output — the installation manual governs.[^s4]

## Key takeaways

- Comfort outdoors comes in layers: wind break, overhead cover, then radiant heat aimed at people.
- Radiant heat warms people, not air, so it keeps working in light wind; forced air does not.[^s4]
- Short and medium wave heaters warm in seconds — right for a patio used on impulse.
- Plan heat with the pergola and lighting, in the first budget, so wiring and recessing are built in.
- Every heater recommendation includes its controls; app and voice features need compatible Affinity controls, sold separately.

## FAQ

### Can you use a patio in winter?
Yes, in most US climates, if the seating is sheltered from wind and warmed by radiant heat. Colder regions may need more heaters for the same area; coverage varies with mounting height, environment and installation.[^s5]

### Do patio heaters work in wind?
Radiant heaters do, in light to mild wind, because they warm people and surfaces directly. Long-wave heaters are the most wind-sensitive.[^s4] See [do infrared heaters work in wind](item:RES-CO-KB1).

### Is a fire pit enough to keep a patio warm?
For the people facing it, briefly. A fire pit is atmosphere; radiant heaters aimed at the seating keep everyone warm for the whole evening.

### Does a covered patio need fewer heaters?
Often it helps: a partial cover holds a heat cloud over the space. The space must stay at least 30% open to count as outdoor.[^s4]

### What should I plan first, the patio or the heat?
Both together. Wiring, gas lines and recesses are easiest while the structure is open.
