To enjoy your patio when the air turns cool, shelter it from the wind, add something overhead, and warm the people directly. Radiant (infrared) heaters take care of that last part, warming skin and furniture the way the sun does, so they keep working in an open space where warm air would simply drift away.[^s4] Plan all three together and an evening outside lasts hours longer, from autumn well into spring.

## Why So Many Patios Fall Quiet After Dark

Most new homes now come with a patio, and far fewer come with the warmth to enjoy it once the sun goes down. 60.5% of single-family homes started in 2025 had a patio, and the average new-home patio is about 320 ft².[^s1][^s2] Yet in Bromic's own homeowner survey, 94% said their outdoor space could be used more, and fewer than one in four (23%) put heating into the plan at the start.[^s3] [[VERIFY: survey base, fieldwork dates and question wording for the 94% and 23% figures — Marketing]]

You may know the pattern well. Dinner moves inside at sunset, and the lounge set that was so inviting in July is covered by October. What changes that is comfort that holds as the temperature drops, far more than another piece of furniture.

## Layering Comfort Into Your Outdoor Space

Each layer plays its own part, and the table sets them out by what each one changes.

| Layer | What it does | Works in wind? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind break (screen, wall, planting) | Stops moving air stripping heat off skin | Yes – that is its job | Keep the space outdoor: at least 30% of the perimeter permanently open[^s4] |
| Overhead cover (pergola, roof, louvres) | Holds a "heat cloud" over the seating and adds a convective boost[^s4] | Partly | Also gives a mounting surface for built-in heaters |
| Radiant heater, short or medium wave | Warms people and objects directly | Yes, in light to mild wind[^s4] | Short wave warms almost instantly; medium wave in about 30 seconds[^s4] |
| Fire pit or fireplace | Atmosphere and a local hot spot | Poorly | Warms whoever faces it; smoke follows the breeze |
| Forced-air or convection heater | Heats air | No | Bromic's guidance: forced air does not work outdoors[^s4] |
| Blankets, cushions, warm lighting | Comfort at the edges | n/a | Best alongside heat, not instead of it |

## Warmth That Feels Like Sunlight

Outdoors there is no room of air to hold the heat, so anything that warms the air loses it to the first breeze. Infrared heat travels as radiation and is absorbed by whatever it reaches – your shoulders, the table, the stone floor – which then stays pleasantly warm.[^s4]

Infrared comes in different wavelengths, and Bromic's planning guide sets them out: short wave is about 90% radiant and warms instantly; medium wave is about 60% radiant with a roughly 30-second warm-up; long wave is only about 40% radiant, takes around five minutes to warm up, and is the most affected by wind.[^s4] On a patio you step out to on a whim, that warm-up time makes all the difference.

## Bringing Heat Into The Plan Early

The best moment is before anything is built. Heating planned in from the first drawing costs less and sits more beautifully in the finished space:

- **Wiring and gas** are simplest to run while walls and ceilings are open. Adding them afterwards can mean channelling stone or patching a finished soffit.
- **Recessed heaters** need a cavity planned into the ceiling, where recess kits flush-mount electric units.[^s4]
- **Mounting heights** decide where heaters can go. Bromic guidance: wall mounts at least 72" above the floor, ceiling mounts at least 96", varying with output – the installation manual governs.[^s4]

## What To Take Away

- Comfort outdoors comes in layers: a wind break, overhead cover, then radiant heat aimed at the people.
- Radiant heat warms people directly, so it keeps working in light wind where forced air struggles.[^s4]
- Short and medium wave heaters warm in seconds, which suits a patio you step out to on impulse.
- Plan heat with the pergola and lighting, in the first budget, so wiring and recessing are built in.
- Every heater recommendation includes its controls, and app and voice features need compatible Affinity controls, sold separately.

## FAQ

### Can you use a patio in winter?
Yes, in most US climates, when the seating is sheltered from wind and warmed by radiant heat. Colder regions may need more heaters for the same area, and coverage varies with mounting height, environment and installation.[^s5]

### Do patio heaters work in wind?
Radiant heaters do, in light to mild wind, because they warm people and surfaces directly. Long-wave heaters are the most wind-sensitive.[^s4] See [do infrared heaters work in wind](item:RES-CO-KB1).

### Is a fire pit enough to keep a patio warm?
For the people facing it, for a while. A fire pit brings atmosphere, and radiant heaters aimed at the seating keep everyone warm through the whole evening.

### Does a covered patio need fewer heaters?
It often helps, because a partial cover holds a heat cloud over the space. The space must stay at least 30% open to count as outdoor.[^s4]

### What should I plan first, the patio or the heat?
Plan them together. Wiring, gas lines and recesses are easiest to place while the structure is open.
