Set the height from the people the heater serves. Our guidance is about 8 ft over a standard dining table, about 10 ft over high-tops and bar counters, and about 12 ft where guests stand or walk through, roughly 6 ft above their heads[^s1]. Each sits above Bromic's minimums of 72 in on a wall and 96 in on a ceiling[^s2], and the model's installation manual has the final word.

| How the space is used | Mounting height (Bromic guidance)[^s1] | Heater to head, approx. |
|---|---|---|
| Seated at a dining or lounge table | About 8 ft | About 4 ft |
| Seated at a high-top or bar | About 10 ft | Close to a seated table, without crowding a taller guest |
| Standing, queuing or passing through | About 12 ft | About 6 ft |

[[VERIFY: 8 / 10 / 12 ft heights by use against each model's manual and coverage data — Product team]]

## Why A Few Feet Change The Evening

Radiant warmth thins as it spreads. For a small source, twice the distance delivers about a quarter of the heat[^s3], so a heater at 12 ft over a dining table puts roughly double the distance between itself and a seated guest compared with one at 8 ft. Linear heaters fall off less steeply close in, so read this as direction and take design values from each model's coverage data[^s4]. The same applies across the floor: a chair 12 ft from a wall heater feels less warmth than one at 10 ft at the same height[^s1].

## Designing Around The Wall-Mount Dead Zone

- **Keep the table out from the wall.** A table set directly beneath a wall heater sits below its beam, in a dead zone where little warmth lands[^s1]. Move it into the throw of the heater.
- **Warm both sides.** One wall heater warms the guests facing it; a heater on each side of the table gives every seat its share, the optimal zone[^s1].
- **Plan for the coldest night you will use.** Colder air shrinks the area a heater keeps comfortable[^s1], so size for it, then dim down on milder evenings.
