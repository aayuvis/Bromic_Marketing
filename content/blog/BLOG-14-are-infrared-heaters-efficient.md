Yes. Outdoors, infrared is the efficient way to heat, because it warms people and surfaces rather than air that rises and blows away[^s2]. An electric heater turns all the electricity it draws into heat[^s1]; the difference between heaters is how much of that heat reaches the people you are warming. That depends on how radiant the heater is, where it is aimed, how it is controlled, and whether it is sized for the space.

## What does "efficient" mean for an outdoor heater?

Indoors, a heater warms the air in a room and the walls keep it. Outside, that warm air leaves[^s2]. So the useful measure is not how much energy becomes heat, which for an electric resistance heater is all of it[^s1], but how much arrives where people sit.

The radiant share answers that. Bromic's Planning Guide rates short wave the most efficient, at about 90% radiant, and long wave the least, at about 40%, with the rest leaving as warm air[^s2].

| Choice | Wastes more | Wastes less |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | Long wave, about 40% radiant[^s2] | Short or medium wave, 90% or 60% radiant[^s2] |
| Direction | Heat sent equally in all directions | Directional heat aimed at the seating[^s2] |
| Control | On or off only, left running | Dimming, zones and schedules |
| Sizing | Too few heaters run flat out, or too many | Coverage matched to where people sit |

The live version of this post said infrared heaters use about 30% less energy than traditional heating. We could not find a source for that figure, so it is removed.

## How much does an infrared heater cost to run?

For electric, multiply the heater's output in kilowatts by your electricity rate:

**cost per hour = kW × $/kWh**

At the US average residential rate of about 18.3¢/kWh[^s3], a 3,400 W Platinum Smart-Heat Electric heater costs about 3.4 × $0.183 = $0.62 an hour at full output. Turned down, it costs less. Your own bill decides the real number. [[VERIFY: 2026 rates — Brian Kim/Finance]]

For gas, divide the heater's BTU per hour by 100,000 and multiply by your price per therm; for propane, divide by 91,500 and multiply by your price per gallon. The full workings are in [how much it costs to run an outdoor heater](item:RES-IN-01).

## Four ways to waste less heat

1. **Choose a high radiant share.** Short and medium wave put most of their energy into radiation; long wave loses most of it to the air[^s2].
2. **Aim it.** Directional heaters push heat one way, toward the guests, and avoid over-heating the people closest[^s2]. Mount and angle for the seating, not the middle of the floor.
3. **Control output, not air temperature.** Outdoor heat cannot be thermostatically controlled[^s2]. Affinity Smart-Heat Dimmer controllers dim compatible electric heaters in 1% steps[^s5], Eclipse steps between 33, 66 and 100%[^s4], and the 4,000 and 6,000 W Tungsten Electric have two separately controlled elements[^s5]. The Smart-Heat app adds zones, schedules and timers, so heat runs only where and when people are[^s4].
4. **Size it.** Coverage per heater varies with model, mounting height, environment and installation[^s4]. A layout matched to the seating avoids both cold spots and heaters that warm empty corners.

## Frequently asked

### Do infrared heaters use a lot of electricity?
They use exactly their rated output at full power: a 3,400 W heater draws 3.4 kWh an hour. Dimming and scheduling cut that. Multiply by your rate for the cost.

### Is electric or gas infrared more efficient?
Both can be infrared. Electric converts all its energy to heat at the heater[^s1] and suits covered spaces; gas suits larger open areas. Running cost depends on local energy prices; see [gas or electric](https://www.bromic.com/blog/gas-or-electric-patio-heaters-which-one-is-right-for-me/).

### What is the most efficient way to heat a patio?
A radiant, directional heater aimed at the seating, sized for the space and run through dimming or schedules[^s2].

### Can I put an outdoor heater on a thermostat?
Not usefully. Outdoor air temperature cannot be held[^s2]; control output with an Affinity controller instead.
